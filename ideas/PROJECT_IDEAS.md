# A first paper: friction-consistent motion for a ballbot

Memo for the collaboration. The numbers are from `results/STUDY.md`, regenerated by `python3 -m experiments.run_study`.

## Do this, and keep it small

The publishable claim is not "we balanced a ballbot" and not "we added another obstacle-avoidance MPC." Both are crowded. The claim is:

> A lean limit is not a floor-friction limit. On a ballbot that measures position from its rollers, a plan that respects tilt and ignores μ slips, the odometry diverges, and the robot falls on floors where a plan capped by μ still rolls without slip.

The controller family is one law with a floor-dependent acceleration:

```
a_max = min( a_steady(6°),  0.65 * μ * g )
```

and a torque projection that keeps the shear required for rolling inside `μ N`. When even the projected torque cannot catch the lean, the maneuver is infeasible and the legs should come down. Three regimes, one controller. That is the μ-dependent control, made specific.

## What is already done

Read `LITERATURE.md` for the order. The short version:

- CMU, 2006–2014 (Hollis, Kantor, Nagarajan). The machine, the inverse mouse-ball drive, LQR balance, and shape-space planning. Shape-space planning maps a desired base acceleration to a lean trajectory through the no-slip dynamic constraint. Our parameters are their Table I.
- ETH Rezero (Fankhauser and Gwerder, 2010) and Tohoku BallIP (Kumagai and Ochiai). Three omniwheels instead of the mouse-ball drive. Same planar balance idea.
- Minniti, Farshidian, Grandia, Hutter, 2019. Whole-body MPC on Rezero with an arm. Still a rolling model.
- Xiao, Mansouri, Lam, Ramos, Hsiao-Wecksler, 2023. A modern high-payload drivetrain.
- Salehi, 2025. RL for uneven terrain. The paper says outright that control-theoretic ballbot navigation usually assumes no slip.
- Wan et al., asRoBallet, RSS 2026. The closest neighbor. Friction-aware RL, deployed on a humanoid ballbot. They model roller-on-ball and ball-on-floor tribology and randomize sliding μ over about 0.6–1.2. That is a high band, used as domain randomization so a policy survives the reality gap. It is not a planner that changes its acceleration because the floor changed.
- Vu, Nguyen, and their coauthors at Hanoi, 2024–2026, including Safe-Ballbot (arXiv, November 2026). Tilt-consistent MPC with control barrier functions. Their acceleration cap comes from the largest steady lean the balance layer will accept. Floor friction is not in that cap. This is the planner to extend, not to re-implement.
- Tafrishi, Svinin, and Tahara, 2025. A survey of rolling-contact planning. Ballbots are filed under high friction and a no-slip model.

The Nguyen group is publishing obstacle-avoidance MPC for ballbots quickly. A paper that is "CBF-MPC, but ours" will read as a follow-up to them. asRoBallet owns "close the friction reality gap with RL and show hardware." Leave both of those alone.

## The result we already have

Planar model, CMU parameters, 2 m goal, feedback from the roller encoder `x = r ψ`. Same inner LQR. Only the outer command and the floor change.

- μ = 1.0. Step LQR, tilt-limited, and friction-aware all arrive, no slip.
- μ = 0.8. Tilt-limited and friction-aware arrive. The step command still falls. Its encoder ends 8.6 m off.
- μ from 0.08 through 0.5. Friction-aware arrives with zero time in slip and the encoder on top of the truth. Peak lean stays between 4.5° and 7.5°. Step and tilt-limited slip within the first second, the encoder runs to between −28 m and +50 m, and both fall.
- μ = 0.06. Friction-aware also falls, with zero slip. Clipping the torque to the cone avoids the skid and still cannot produce the balancing moment. That is the refusal region.

Standing up from a lean, with the true position given to the controller, is a different and much easier problem. The recovery map in `results/recovery_map.png` is dark (recovered) down to μ = 0.05 for leans of a few degrees. Friction becomes the story when the robot has to move and has to trust its rollers.

In steady rolling, `|f| / N = |a| / g` exactly, because the normal force is the weight. A 6° lean on this machine is a steady acceleration of about 0.88 m/s², which is a steady μ of about 0.09. The tilt-limited law still fails up through μ = 0.5 because the transient that acquires the lean, and the encoder error that follows the first slip, demand more than that steady value. Quoting `|a| < μ g` from the steady formula is the right constraint and it is not, by itself, a sufficient controller.

MuJoCo, same three laws, sphere on a plane:

- μ = 1. All three arrive at a 1.5 m goal.
- μ = 0.12. All three fall. Friction-aware gets to about 1.85 m before the lean runs away. Step falls immediately. The torque clip is computed from the rigid planar model; once MuJoCo's compliant contact is sliding, that clip is solving the wrong instantaneous problem, and the encoder hides the slip from the feedback. This is the hole in the present controller. It should be in the paper as a limitation, or closed before submission. It should not be omitted.

A noise-free probe recovers μ exactly. During sliding, `f = (τ − I_b ψ̈) / r` and `|f| = μ N`, so the estimate matches the true coefficient to solver accuracy (`results/mu_from_slip.png`). On hardware the same formula is the estimator. The work is filtering `ψ̈` and deciding that slip has started.

## The paper

Working title: *Friction-consistent motion planning for a ballbot, and the odometry failure when the floor is ignored.*

Contributions, in the order a reviewer will check:

1. The steady relation `μ = |a| / g`, and the separate lean map from `a` to θ for this class of robot. State both. They answer different questions.
2. The three-regime law above, including an explicit infeasible region.
3. The encoder experiment: same law with roller feedback, across μ. The figure is `results/timeseries_mu0.15.png` against `timeseries_mu1.00.png`.
4. Either a MuJoCo (or hardware) confirmation, or a clear statement that the rigid projection is not sufficient under compliant contact, plus the fix.

What would make it a journal paper rather than a workshop note is one real floor change: tile, and the same tile with a low-friction sheet, with the rollers as the only position sensor. Simulation-only ballbot papers exist, including several of the 2024–2025 CBF papers. A floor change is the difference between "we found a bug in the standard assumption" and "we measured it."

If neither of you has a ballbot, say so early. Building one is a different project. Borrowing a few hours on someone else's machine, or renting the comparison as a simulation paper with the MuJoCo hole closed, are the two honest paths. asRoBallet's group has hardware and just published the RL version of the friction question; a model-based planner that cites them and does something they did not do is a collaboration to consider, not a territory to pretend is empty.

## Later projects, in order

1. **Close the MuJoCo gap.** Detect slip from the residual between the no-slip prediction and the encoder, and drop `a_max` when the residual grows, using only signals a robot has. Re-run μ = 0.12. If the friction-aware law then arrives and the other two still fall, the planar result has survived contact compliance.
2. **μ is unknown at the start.** The probe we have is algebraic. Add encoder quantization and gyro noise, ramp a gentle acceleration until slip onset, and use the estimate for the rest of the move. Report the one-sided case: if the probe never slips, you only know a lower bound.
3. **Shape-space planning inside the cone.** Nagarajan's planner inverts the no-slip constraint. Outside the cone that constraint is gone and the force saturates. A hybrid planner with a stick mode and a saturated mode is the theoretical version of contribution 2, and it cites CMU directly. Do this after the experiment is stable, or it becomes a theorem with no failure case.
4. **Two friction coefficients, not one.** Wheel-on-ball slip looks like a loss of torque. Ball-on-floor slip looks like odometry error. They need different reactions. asRoBallet already builds the contact. Use their model. Do not start by re-deriving roller geometry.
5. **Rolling resistance versus sliding μ.** Carpet can have a high sliding μ and a large rolling resistance, so constant speed needs a steady lean that polished concrete does not. Identify both. This is a second paper if the first one lands.

Skip, for this collaboration: another balancing PID, a pure RL balance demo, and an obstacle-avoidance race with Vu and Nguyen.

## Two ballbots as a walking aid

This waits on the first paper. It is the reason a reviewer might care about the cone. It is not the paper in which the cone is derived.

The picture is one ballbot at each hand, for someone relearning a step after a stroke or a leg injury. Not a walker frame. Each machine is a rolling support the person holds, and the pair has to agree on how much force each side may give. The geometry that matters first is frontal: the person tips toward the weak side, and the ballbot on that side is asked to push back. Forward walking is the second problem.

A ballbot with no hand on it cannot brace. At rest the horizontal equation says the friction force is zero, so the motor torque is zero and the lean is zero. A hand changes that. The person pushes on the handle, the ballbot leans away from the load, and it can stand still. The friction that keeps the ball from sliding is the support force. On this CMU mass the weight is about 530 N, so a floor with μ = 0.15 can hold about 80 N of horizontal help. A sideways catch is often more than that. Weight the person sets down on the handle increases the normal force and therefore the friction budget, and it also increases the moment the motor must hold.

So the project is a force budget, shared by two robots. Each ballbot advertises the largest horizontal force its own floor will still support, μ N, after the torque that keeps it upright is paid. The pair splits the push the person's lean is asking for. If the weak side is on a smoother patch, that machine lowers its share and the other takes what it can. If neither cone can hold the ask, both refuse and the person is cued to stop. A support that slips is a falling mass next to the person. The encoder failure from the first paper is the dangerous version of the same bug: the ballbot believes it is braced, and it is sliding into them.

The drive for a clinic aisle is three omniwheels, BallIP or Rezero size, with the handles at hand height. The inverse mouse-ball machine is a large body for that aisle. The planar torque model remains the balance layer. The new contact is the hand. The new coordination is one requested force split across two cones.

The experiment that belongs in a paper is not a clinical trial. A healthy person, one knee braced, two handles with load cells, tile under both machines and then a low-friction sheet under one. Record the force each side produced against the force it promised. The result to claim is that the promise stayed inside the cone, the load moved to the other side when one floor changed, and the person did not have to catch a falling robot. A gait lab is the way to get the person, the cameras, and the ethics review. Building two ballbots is a different project. The model to write first is the planar robot, twice, with the hand force added to the three equations of motion, and a person as an inverted pendulum between the handles.
